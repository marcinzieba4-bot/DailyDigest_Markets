🇨🇳 China  Mixed  stimulus-led, data still soft

Macro Stance: China is trying to buy growth with the budget while the price data and the trade data both point the other way. Consumer prices have dropped below zero for the first time in a year [34], which is the deflation problem Reuters said policy has so far only talked about: 'more words than deeds' on consumption [35]. The National People's Congress set the growth goal at around 5% [36] and the budget deficit at around 4% of GDP, a record [37]. Officials say there is room for more stimulus if growth slows [38], and economists told CNBC that a much stronger package is needed if the trade war intensifies [39]. The policy signal is clear. The question for the next two quarters is whether the spending reaches households and private demand, or stays in infrastructure and the state sector.

PMIs & Activity: The latest activity survey is the official non-manufacturing PMI, which was 50.4 in February [40], a reading just above the expansion line rather than a strong one. Trade is the weak leg. China's exports missed forecasts as US tariffs bit, and imports recorded their sharpest decline since July 2023 [41]. The Shanghai Composite closed at 3,372.55 index points, up 2.08% over 20 sessions, which says equity investors are reading the policy turn more generously than the activity data allows (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-03-07). The national legislature opened its annual session with the message that the economy is stable [42], but the trade numbers and the deflation print are the evidence that will be read against it.

Credit Impulse: Credit impulse is being delivered through the budget more than through bank lending. The official budget deficit has been raised to its highest level in more than 30 years [43], and the finance minister has said China has more room to act on fiscal policy amid global uncertainties [44]. The widened fiscal window is the stated tool [45], and the state planner is setting up a national venture-capital guidance fund [46]. The banking-side signal is less encouraging. The developer Longfor's bonds sank deeper into junk territory [47], which tells you the property credit channel is still impaired. What would confirm the view is a monthly credit print in which household borrowing turns up while developer credit keeps shrinking, and until that appears the impulse is fiscal rather than private.

Property: Property is the sector that policy most wants to stabilise, and officials are claiming progress. A minister said the property sector is showing positive changes [48], and Global Times reported that the real-estate market keeps stabilising as policy support lifts confidence [49]. The concrete step under discussion is ending the price cap on local governments buying apartments, which Bloomberg reported Beijing is weighing [50]. The caution is that the stabilisation claim sits alongside the developer bond stress at Longfor [47] and luxury retailers closing more China outlets as spending shrinks [51]. Property is the place where the weak household balance sheet shows up most clearly, and it is the reason the consumer-price print matters more than any single activity survey.

Fiscal, Policy & Trade: Fiscal policy is expanding on several fronts at once, and trade policy is pushing the other way. The defence budget is rising 7.2% [52], and business coverage says military spending is growing faster than the economy [53]. China is boosting food imports from Latin America and Europe to offset the US trade war [54], and it hit Canadian farm and food products with retaliatory tariffs [55] after hitting US agriculture [56]. Beijing also added 15 US companies to its export-control list [57]. On the supply side the state is cutting steel output to restructure the sector [58], which matters for commodity demand. The central bank continued buying gold, adding to reserves for a fourth straight month in February [59]. None of these measures is a demand-side stimulus on its own; they are the policy toolkit being set out in the same week.

Markets & Flows: Equity markets have moved well ahead of the macro numbers. The Hang Seng closed at 24,231.30, down 0.57% in the latest session but up 5.62% over five sessions and 14.66% over 20 (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-03-07). The China internet ETF KWEB is at 36.99 USD, up 16.28% over 20 sessions, and the CSI 300 ETF ASHR is at 27.03 USD, up 3.56% (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-03-07). Alibaba is the clearest single name: its ADR is at 140.62 USD, up 40.09% over 20 sessions (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-03-07), after the company unveiled a reasoning model that sent the shares higher [60]. Hong Kong stocks capped their best week in two months on policy support [61]. The yuan is steady: USD/CNY is at 7.2506, down 0.51% over 20 sessions (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-03-07). Goldman raised its emerging-market target on the AI-driven China rally [62].

New / Most Important: The signal that matters most is the gap between the equity rally and the price data. Consumer prices turned negative [34], the export and import numbers are weak [41], and the property signals are mixed [48][47]. Against that, the equity market has priced a policy put with real conviction, and the Alibaba move is a sentiment indicator as much as a fundamental one [63]. The retaliatory tariff exchanges with Canada and the US show that trade friction is spreading beyond Washington [64]. The new information in the set is that officials are now openly discussing more stimulus if growth slows [38], which is a policy reaction to the weak data rather than a sign that the data is already turning.

Playbook ✓: Hold China exposure through the internet and policy-sensitive names rather than the broad consumer or property complex. The trade is a fiscal-liquidity story with a deflation cap: buy the dips in the state-backed and platform names while the stimulus language keeps rising, and keep property exposure small until the developer credit channel stops shrinking. A move in USD/CNY back toward 7.29, the level 20 sessions earlier (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-02-07), would signal that the policy easing is being offset by capital outflow, and the position should be cut. What would confirm the broader recovery is a property stabilisation that shows up in developer credit and an improvement in the export data. ⚠ Divergence: the Hang Seng is up 14.66% over 20 sessions while consumer prices have turned negative and exports missed forecasts, so the rally is pricing a policy outcome that the activity data has not yet delivered.

New / Most Important: The signal that matters most is that the Warsaw market is now being led by a combination of the strong zloty, foreign bond buying and defence procurement. The second signal is that the market is pricing the zloty's strength as a sign of confidence, while the exporters carry the cost [95].

Playbook ✓: Own the Polish market through the banks and the energy and defence names, but keep the currency leg as the hedge rather than the bet. The trade is a yield-and-currency story with a defence kicker: buy the WIG20 tracker on dips while USD/PLN stays below the 4.03 level it held 20 sessions earlier (Yahoo Finance, prior close 2025-02-07), and add to Orlen and the banks on the index rebalancing if the flows confirm it. Cut the position if the Ukraine talks turn into a peace framework, because the defence names carry the premium and would reprice first, and if the central bank's tone turns dovish while the zloty is still strong. ⚠ Divergence: the zloty is up sharply against the dollar and the WIG20 is up 6.15% over 20 sessions, but the same strength is the headwind that Santander flags for exporters and GDP, so the equity rally is running ahead of the growth signal.

The dominant popular-narrative trade is Germany's rearmament, which is treated like a picks-and-shovels capex wave and which moves with the mechanics of the GameStop effect that Reuters tied to Eutelsat's 650% surge [263], the same reflexive pattern that drives any buildout-led speculative run.

Historical Analog: The mechanical analogy is the GameStop squeeze, which Reuters named in its piece on Eutelsat [263]. The first wave is a real repricing: a credible policy change moves the cash flows of a set of companies, and the shares re-rate on the news. The second wave is retail, which arrives once the first move is visible on a chart and the story is repeated enough times to feel like a sure thing. What makes it dangerous is that the second wave buys the second derivative of the news, the expected order book, rather than the orders themselves, so the first disappointment on delivery hits the most crowded names first.

The disagreement is about pace, not direction: the spending itself is on the record, but the sceptics think the orders arrive later than the price assumes.
