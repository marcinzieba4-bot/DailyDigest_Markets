🇺🇸 United States  Mixed  ↑ rates — hiking cycle, pause debated

Macro Stance: The Fed remains in a tightening posture, though multiple officials this week signaled the next rate increase can wait. Jefferson said the Fed may need more time to weigh its next move. Williams sees one more hike in late 2026. The economy added only 29,000 jobs in September — far fewer than expected — which markets interpreted as reducing near-term hike urgency. The 10Y yield surged to 5.28% (+52 bps vs 4W ago), and the 2Y sits at 4.83% (prior close, +49 bps vs 4W ago), with the curve at +45 bps — positively sloped but still flat by historical standards. The 2s-10s holding in positive territory, little changed over four weeks, while both ends climb sharply is the signature of a broad rate repricing, not a growth scare.

New / Most Important: The September payrolls miss (29K) was the dominant market event this week. Paradoxically, stocks rose on the print — the S&P 500 rallied toward records — because markets read weakness as constraining Fed hawks. But bonds did NOT rally; yields continued surging. This is the critical divergence: equities and bonds are pricing contradictory futures. The NYT flagged a "powerful yet fragile force propping up stocks," and the WSJ highlighted an "amazing stat that's a red flag." The AI wealth effect (Axios) and continued AI capex (Deloitte) remain the narrow pillars supporting mega-cap leadership, with the Russell/Nasdaq ratio collapsing 8.7% vs 4W ago — extreme narrowness.

Playbook ✓: Late-cycle narrow leadership concentrated in AI/mega-cap exactly as expected; VIX at 15.31 despite surging yields signals complacency.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The bond market is breaking from the equity script in a structurally important way. A 10Y at 5.28% and rising should be compressing equity multiples, yet the Nasdaq 100 is up 4.5% over four weeks. This can persist only if AI earnings growth is perceived as immune to discount-rate mechanics — a fragile assumption. The more structural concern is that term premium is repricing for fiscal reasons (deficit trajectory, Treasury supply) rather than growth expectations, which means the usual "bad economic data → lower yields → equity support" transmission mechanism is broken. If the labor market continues softening while yields keep climbing, the equity market will eventually have to arbitrate between the growth story and the cost-of-capital reality. Russell 2000 down 4.6% suggests that arbitration has already begun outside mega-cap.

🇪🇺 European Union  Bearish  ↑ rates — ECB under pressure to hike further

Macro Stance: The ECB faces a re-acceleration problem. Eurozone inflation surged to 3.8% — a three-year high — well above target. Energy prices are being flagged by top ECB policymakers as threatening to bleed into broader inflation. The ECB remains in a tightening bias with officials stating they "cannot wait" for pass-through effects.

New / Most Important: The inflation print at 3.8% was the week's dominant signal, arriving hotter than expected and putting paid to any near-term pivot hopes. Multiple Reuters and FT headlines confirmed this was an upside surprise, intensifying pressure on the ECB to hike again. This is a structural headwind for European equities, particularly rate-sensitive sectors, and widens the policy divergence with the US, where the Fed is at least pausing to assess. For European credit and sovereign spreads, the combination of hiking into slowing growth is the classic stagflationary squeeze that historically punishes peripheral bonds.

Playbook ✓: Higher-for-longer European rates with sticky services inflation — consensus has been positioned for this.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The divergence is that energy-driven inflation is re-accelerating even as European industrial activity remains weak. This is a supply-side inflation impulse that monetary tightening cannot efficiently address, yet the ECB is being forced to hike anyway due to mandate. This creates a policy-error risk where overtightening crushes demand without solving the inflation source. For global capital flows, the implication is that European assets become doubly unattractive — rising rates hurt duration, while the growth slowdown hurts earnings. Expect continued outflows from European equities toward US mega-cap, reinforcing the narrow-market dynamic visible in the Russell/Nasdaq ratio.
