🇺🇸 United States  Mixed  → rates on hold / higher for longer

Macro Stance: The Fed remains in a restrictive posture with the 10Y yield surging to 5.23% (+23 bps vs 4 weeks ago) and the 2Y at 4.80% (prior close, +17 bps vs 4W). The curve at +44 bps (prior close, 11 bps steeper than four weeks ago) is positive but still flat by historical standards — no longer recessionary, but the absolute level of long-end rates is punishing. VIX at 15.42 (↓10.3%) signals complacency despite one of the highest nominal yield environments in over a decade. The Nasdaq 100 is powering ahead at 30,515 (↑5.5%) while the Russell 2000 at 2,783 (↓3.1%) flags a historically narrow market — the Russell/Nasdaq ratio has plunged 8.1% in 4 weeks.

New / Most Important: JPMorgan posted earnings today with profits missing analyst estimates; Jamie Dimon warned that "economic forces shift under the surface." The IMF flagged risks from an AI market correction, war, and debt. Fed's Hammack warned inflation may persist. The September CPI report is in focus. Meanwhile, the election narrative is heating up with Morningstar and multiple outlets framing November's implications for markets.

Playbook ✓: AI concentration driving mega-cap outperformance while small-caps lag is the consensus trade, and it's working — Nasdaq +5.5% vs Russell −3.1% in four weeks is textbook narrow liquidity funneling. Big bank earnings season started with mixed-but-resilient results.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The structural break is the 10Y at 5.23% coexisting with a S&P 500 at all-time highs near 7,800. This is deeply anomalous by any historical standard. In prior cycles, sustained 10Y yields above 5% cratered equity valuations — here the equity risk premium is effectively zero or negative for the broad market, and mega-caps are trading as duration instruments despite rising duration costs. The explanation is that AI capex flows are being treated as immune to discount rate sensitivity — a form of "productive bubble" where the cash flow generation is real enough to justify ignoring the cost of capital. The danger is that the Russell 2000's collapse reveals the true rate sensitivity of the economy: small and mid-cap companies cannot refinance at these levels. If bank earnings commentary continues to flag "shifting undercurrents" while loan growth stalls, the transmission from high rates into credit stress will come through commercial real estate and leveraged middle-market borrowers — not through the S&P headline.
