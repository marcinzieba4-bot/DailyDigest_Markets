🇪🇺 European Union  Bearish  ↑ hawkish pivot risk

Macro Stance: The ECB is in a policy limbo that is rapidly becoming a structural problem. Lagarde said the ECB doesn't need to fight inflation with "same force" as 2022-23, signaling comfort with the current stance. But ECB's Wunsch said another hike might be needed, Apollo's Slok flagged a possible September hike, and Bundesbank's Nagel warned inflation will stay "well above target." This is the most hawkish cacophony from the ECB in months.

New / Most Important: Flash inflation data from top eurozone economies showed slowing but remains above target — the Bundesbank's explicit warning that it stays "well above" is the critical signal. Lagarde simultaneously said Europe's economic resilience gives the ECB "greater room to move rates" — a constructive-sounding statement that actually opens the door to tightening, not easing. The euro is underperforming ahead of German HICP data, which is unusual if the market believed the easing narrative. This is a structurally important shift: the ECB consensus has flipped from "next move is a cut" to "next move could be a hike" in the span of two weeks. For cross-border flows, a hawkish ECB repricing narrows the US-EU rate differential, which should weaken USD/EUR — but the euro is falling anyway, suggesting capital is fleeing Europe on growth fears, not chasing yield. This creates a toxic combination: tighter money into a fragile growth environment. European bonds need close monitoring for a Q3 selloff.

Playbook ✓: Inflation stickiness in services sector continuing to frustrate policymakers — entirely on script.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: Euro weakening despite hawkish ECB repricing is the key break — it means the capital flow story is dominating the rate differential story, which is bearish for European risk assets.

Macro Stance: The PBOC's new overnight framework is now live. After debuting the overnight reverse repo at 1.25% (Reuters, [Jun 28]), it doubled the overnight cash injection while keeping the rate unchanged (Reuters, [Jun 29]); Bloomberg notes the surprise that no rate was formally released ([Jun 29]). With loan prime rates still on hold, the easing is happening at the very front end and through volume, not through a headline cut. The yuan fixing was weakened for a fourth session as the dollar advanced (Bloomberg, [Jun 23]), a managed drift against a dollar near 13-month highs, while Euronews details how the currency widens the EU's deficit ([Jun 23]); T. Rowe Price sees the yuan 1%-2% weaker by year-end (TradingView, [Jun 26]). Prices are split: May CPI rose only 1.2% (China Daily, [Jun 10]) while factory-gate inflation hit a near four-year high (Reuters, [Jun 9]).

PMIs & Activity: The growth pulse turned up in June. The official manufacturing PMI rose to 50.3 (Reuters, [Jun 29]), back into expansion from a flat 50 in May (Reuters, [May 30]) and faster than expected on tech export demand (CNBC, [Jun 29]); the non-manufacturing PMI rose to 50.2 (Reuters, [Jun 29]). The composition matters: Bloomberg frames it as a return to growth because exports are booming ([Jun 29]), and ING still expects a second-quarter slowdown ([Jun 29]). The May hard data show why: industrial output growth quickened to 4.5% (China Daily, [Jun 16]), but retail sales fell 0.6%, the first drop in over three years (Il Sole 24 Ore, [Jun 15]), and fixed-asset investment was down 4.1% in the first five months (Macau Business, [Jun 16]). Credit Impulse: Total social financing grew 7.7% in May (CGTN, [Jun 12]) and topped forecasts (Bloomberg, [Jun 12]), but new loans missed as the property slump curbed demand (Reuters, [Jun 12]). The beat is government-bond issuance; private credit demand is still contracting. The PBOC is now urging banks to boost lending because demand remains weak, according to sources (Reuters, [Jun 26]). Household deleveraging continues (SCMP, [Jun 15]). Read: the headline impulse is flat-to-positive, but the household and private-firm impulse is still negative, which is why output does not convert into consumption.

Fiscal, Policy & Trade: Fiscal policy is tightening at the margin: Beijing narrowed its cumulative deficit for the first time since 2023 (Business Times, [Jun 23]), and the 62.5 billion yuan third batch of trade-in funds (Global Times, [Jun 26]) is small against the retail contraction. The State Council reviewed AI, trade and carbon plans (Dimsum Daily, [Jun 29]), with no new demand package. On trade, the EU and China agreed an October deadline to reset ties (Bloomberg, [Jun 29]), with three months of talks over a €360bn deficit (The Guardian, [Jun 29]) and a €3 customs charge on small parcels aimed at cheap Chinese imports (The Guardian, [Jun 29]). Regulators are also clamping down on higher-yielding offshore debt issuance (Bloomberg, [Jun 29]), tightening one more outflow and funding channel.

New / Most Important: The June PMI is the first upturn in the growth pulse in months, and it is export-led. Manufacturing back above 50 on tech export demand, with services barely positive, fits the same split as May: an AI and export engine running hot while household demand and private credit stay weak. Paired with cheaper overnight money and the October EU deadline, the near-term risk for China is not recession but a trade backlash against the very sector that is carrying growth.

Playbook ✓: Property drag, weak consumption, LPR on hold and a managed yuan drift lower are all on script.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The consensus expected a mid-year stimulus pivot; instead the June PMI upturn rests on exports while the credit impulse outside government bonds is negative and the fiscal impulse is narrowing. China is therefore exporting its excess supply rather than absorbing it at home. The transmission runs through three channels. European exporters face a €360bn deficit and a weak yuan, which is why Brussels has set an October deadline. Bulk-commodity demand tied to construction is fading with the investment slump, a drag on AUD and iron ore that an export-led PMI does not offset. And global goods disinflation is the channel Pimco expects to support EM bonds (Bloomberg, [Jun 23]). A real turn needs loan growth, not bond issuance, to re-accelerate; until it does, rallies in Hong Kong are flows, not fundamentals.

🇯🇵 Japan  Bearish  → dovish revamp / stuck

Macro Stance: The yen has collapsed to a 39-40 year low, breaking 162/USD, and the BOJ just underwent what Reuters calls a "slow, dovish revamp" that casts doubt on any near-term rate hikes. Factory output has slipped. The US-Japan rate gap continues to underpin dollar strength and yen weakness.

New / Most Important: This is a structurally critical moment. The yen at 162 is uncharted territory for the post-Plaza Accord era and represents a regime break in Japanese capital flow dynamics. PM Takaichi's BOJ appointee urged "vigilance" on yen's inflation impact, but Tokyo is keeping its intervention powder dry, with the "line in the sand" apparently shifting higher. The BOJ's dovish personnel revamp makes a July hike increasingly unlikely, which means the carry trade is being re-endorsed by the institution itself. This has massive global implications: Japanese lifers and pension funds running unhedged foreign bond positions at these FX levels face mark-to-market gains that discourage repatriation — meaning Japanese capital stays deployed in US Treasuries and European credit, compressing yields globally. The paradox is that a weaker yen is globally reflationary (cheaper Japanese exports, costlier imports for Japan) while simultaneously acting as a liquidity injection into DM fixed income. If intervention does come, the snap-back in global yields could be severe as repatriation flows reverse.

Playbook ✓: Yen weakness on rate differentials is textbook.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The BOJ's dovish institutional revamp actively undermines its own stated normalization path — the central bank is being restructured to NOT hike, even as imported inflation surges. This is policy incoherence of the highest order.
