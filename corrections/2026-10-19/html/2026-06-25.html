🇺🇸 United States  Mixed  → Hawkish hold

Macro Stance: The Fed under Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish pause at the June meeting, signaling higher-for-longer. The 10Y yield sits at 4.38% (−10 bps vs 4W ago) while the 2Y is at 4.11% (prior close, +10 bps vs 4W), leaving a 30 bps spread — positive but still flat by historical standards, and flattening: long-end demand is pulling the 10Y lower while the front end reprices Fed hawkishness. VIX at 19.15 (↑17.6%) signals rising nervousness. GDP surprised to the upside, durable goods orders declined less than feared, but PCE came in hot on Tuesday.

New / Most Important: The hot PCE print on June 24 is the headline event — it validates the Fed's hawkish stance and kills any near-term rate-cut hopes. Warsh's Fed is openly debating whether to hike, per the Morningstar piece. The DXY surged to 13-month highs this week on the hawkish repricing, strengthening dollar flows. Dimon's comment that this bull market is a "little tsunami" that "will stop" is notable as a contrarian marker from the most-listened-to bank CEO. S&P 500 at 7355 (↓2.2%) and Nasdaq 100 at 29403 (↓1.9%) are pulling back, while Russell 2000 at 3004 (↑2.9%) is outperforming — breadth is expanding into small caps and financials.

Playbook ✓: Hot inflation + hawkish Fed = strong dollar, tech underperformance, financials catching a bid. Russell/Nasdaq ratio ↑4.9% confirms the rotation trade is running as expected.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The divergence is that 10Y yields are actually FALLING despite hot PCE and a hawkish Fed — this is structurally important. The long end is pricing something the front end isn't: either a growth scare beneath the surface, or massive duration demand from pension/sovereign rebalancing flows into quarter-end. When front-end yields rise on hawkish policy but long-end yields fall on a hot inflation print, it typically signals that the bond market sees the Fed making a policy error — tightening into a slowdown that hasn't yet surfaced in headline GDP. The oil crash (WTI at 71.53, ↓19.3% in 4 weeks) and the housing data reinforce this: May housing starts plunged 15.4% to the weakest pace since 2020 and new home sales fell to a four-month low, while the June flash PMI paired a 4-year high in manufacturing with factory jobs at their lowest since 2020. Rate-sensitive demand is rolling over even as services inflation stays sticky. Watch for this growth-inflation disconnect to become the dominant narrative by Q3.

🇪🇺 European Union  Bearish  ↑ Hiking cycle resumed

Macro Stance: The ECB raised rates for the first time since 2023 this cycle, a seismic shift driven by the Middle East energy crisis stoking eurozone inflation. Schnabel stated rates need to rise further to hit the 2% target. Lane described the situation as a "mid-sized inflation shock" requiring a measured response. The euro sank to a one-year low as falling oil prices eased some pressure but came too late to prevent the hike.

New / Most Important: This is structurally new and critically important. The ECB has resumed hiking in mid-2026 — a move almost no one had in their base case at the start of the year. The Middle East crisis (likely the Strait of Hormuz disruption referenced in the Edge Malaysia piece) forced energy prices high enough to reignite eurozone inflation, compelling a policy reversal. The digital euro clearing a key EU legislative hurdle is a secondary but strategically significant development — the explicit goal of reducing dependence on US credit card networks is a de-dollarization move in payments infrastructure. The euro weakening despite rate hikes is the tell: the market views the ECB as hiking into weakness, damaging growth without credibly anchoring inflation expectations. Capital outflows from European equities likely accelerating as the US dollar strengthens simultaneously.

Playbook ✓: Energy shock → inflation → forced tightening → weaker growth is the classic eurozone doom loop, running exactly as scripted.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: Oil prices are now cratering (WTI ↓19.3% in 4 weeks) even as the ECB hikes in response to the energy shock. This creates a bizarre sequencing problem: the ECB is tightening to fight an inflation impulse whose primary driver — energy — is rapidly reversing. If oil stays down, the ECB will have hiked into a disinflationary downdraft, maximizing the growth damage for no inflation benefit. This is the exact opposite of what happened in 2022-23 when they were late. Now they may be late in the other direction — hiking at the peak of the shock. European fixed income could see a violent rally if the ECB is forced to reverse within two quarters.

🇨🇳 China  Mixed  → Easing bias

Macro Stance: The PBOC is easing through plumbing, not price. Loan prime rates were left unchanged for a 13th month in June (Reuters, [Jun 21]), with the 1-year LPR at 3.0% (Mitrade, [Jun 21]). Instead the bank is adding a tool to manage short-term liquidity (Reuters, [Jun 24]) and narrowing its rate corridor, which Capital Economics says comes with "no signs of healthier credit" ([Jun 19]). The governor's line that slower credit growth may be the "new normal" (Caixin, [Jun 18]) says Beijing is not chasing a credit-fuelled rebound. The yuan fixing was weakened for a fourth session as the dollar advanced (Bloomberg, [Jun 23]): a managed drift lower against a dollar at 13-month highs, while Berlin and the ECB openly call the yuan undervalued (Bloomberg, [Jun 19]; Reuters, [Jun 22]). Prices are split. May CPI rose only 1.2% and missed expectations (China Daily, [Jun 10]), while factory-gate inflation hit a near four-year high on Iran-war input costs and the AI boom (Reuters, [Jun 9]).

PMIs & Activity: The official manufacturing PMI stalled at 50 in May (Reuters, [May 30]) with factory orders contracting (Wood Central, [Jun 1]), and the non-manufacturing gauge was barely positive at 50.1 (Macau Business, [May 31]). The private surveys were firmer: manufacturing expanded for a sixth month (Reuters, [May 31]) and services hit 54.4, the fastest pace in three months (TradingView, [Jun 2]). The hard data confirm a two-speed economy. Industrial output growth quickened to 4.5% in May (China Daily, [Jun 16]), but retail sales fell 0.6%, the first drop in over three years (Il Sole 24 Ore, [Jun 15]), and fixed-asset investment was down 4.1% in the first five months (Macau Business, [Jun 16]).

Credit Impulse: Total social financing grew 7.7% in May (CGTN, [Jun 12]) and topped forecasts (Bloomberg, [Jun 12]), but new loans missed as the property slump curbed demand (Reuters, [Jun 12]). The beat is government-bond issuance; private credit demand is still contracting. Bloomberg notes the tilt from loans to bonds ([Jun 17]), and household deleveraging continues (SCMP, [Jun 15]) against a $300 billion pile of bad consumer debt (Bloomberg, [Jun 17]). Read: the headline impulse is flat-to-positive, but the household and private-firm impulse is still negative, which is why output does not convert into consumption.

Property: New home prices fell at a faster pace in May (Reuters, [Jun 15]), a setback to the revival story, even as first-tier new home prices rose for a third month (SCMP, [Jun 16]) and top-city resale prices extended their rebound (Caixin, [Jun 17]). Real-estate investment fell 16.2% y/y in January–May (GMK Center, [Jun 18]), and Bloomberg describes the steel market entering a long plateau after the property crash ([Jun 21]). Tier-1 stabilisation does not offset the national investment slump.

Fiscal, Policy & Trade: The fiscal signal is the surprise. Beijing narrowed its cumulative deficit for the first time since 2023 (Bloomberg, [Jun 22]), against a 2026 deficit ratio of around 4% (China Daily, [Jun 20]). A $9 billion consumer package is being readied (Caixin, [Jun 18]), which is small relative to the retail miss. Exports jumped 19.4% in May (AP, [Jun 9]) and shipments to the US grew 35% (CNBC, [Jun 8]). Euronews details how the yuan widens the EU's trade deficit ([Jun 23]); the EU delayed a confrontation (Financial Times, [Jun 18]) but is preparing tariffs on Chinese plug-in hybrids (Reuters, [Jun 19]). Beijing put 10 US firms, including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, on its export-control list in response to the Pentagon blacklist (Reuters, [Jun 22]), and imposed a 55% tariff on Australian beef once the quota was hit (Global Times, [Jun 19]). The EU trade chief is scheduled to host China's commerce minister next week (Reuters, [Jun 22]).

Markets & Flows: Onshore and offshore have decoupled. Mainland stocks hit a 4-1/2-year high (TradingView, [Jun 22]), led by AI names on demand optimism and policy support (Bloomberg, [Jun 21]). Meanwhile the Hang Seng fell 432 points to breach 23,300 and the HSCEI entered a technical bear market (finance.biggo.com, [Jun 23]), with Bloomberg linking Hong Kong's slide to spending woes ([Jun 23]). The China 10Y yield extended its decline (TradingView, [Jun 18]), consistent with weak loan demand and a bond-heavy credit mix.

New / Most Important: The fiscal-gap cutback is the week's key change. With retail sales contracting, private credit demand shrinking and the PBOC declining to cut, Beijing is choosing restraint over reflation. The growth model is leaning harder on exports and AI manufacturing at the very moment Europe is preparing trade defences.

Playbook ✓: Property drag, weak consumption, LPR on hold and a managed yuan drift lower are all on script.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The consensus expected a mid-year stimulus pivot; instead the credit impulse outside government bonds is negative and the fiscal impulse is narrowing. China is therefore exporting its excess supply rather than absorbing it at home. The transmission runs through three channels. European exporters face a widening bilateral deficit and a weak yuan just as the EU moves on tariffs. Bulk-commodity demand tied to construction is fading, with the steel plateau and an investment slump that hit AUD alongside the beef tariff. And global goods disinflation is the channel Pimco expects to support EM bonds (Bloomberg, [Jun 23]). A real turn needs loan growth, not bond issuance, to re-accelerate; until it does, rallies in Hong Kong are flows, not fundamentals.

🇯🇵 Japan  Bearish  ↑ Hiking — rates at 1%

Macro Stance: The BOJ raised rates to 1%, the highest since 1995, and signaled more hikes ahead. Multiple board members called for faster rate hikes per the June summary. Despite this, the yen continued to sink — intervention risk is elevated with the dollar surging past 1,545 won (Korea proxy suggests broad Asian FX weakness) and an ex-BOJ policymaker warned a Fed hike could push yen to 165.

New / Most Important: This is the most structurally important development in the feed. The BOJ is hiking aggressively — 1% is an enormous move relative to Japan's decades of zero/negative rates — and the yen is STILL falling. This breaks the fundamental model that says higher domestic rates should support the currency. The problem is the carry trade arithmetic: when the Fed is hawkish-holding at a much higher level and the BOJ moves from near-zero to 1%, the rate differential still massively favors dollar longs. Japanese institutional investors (lifers, pensions) repatriating flows should be supporting the yen, but the overwhelming force of global carry capital flowing into the dollar is swamping those flows. The dissent from Takaichi's BOJ pick pushing for faster hikes suggests an internal fracture — the BOJ knows the yen problem is eroding real household purchasing power and stoking imported inflation. Intervention risk is now the dominant near-term tail risk for USDJPY. If the BOJ actually accelerates to 1.25-1.5% while simultaneously intervening, the carry trade unwind could trigger a cross-asset volatility event reminiscent of August 2024.

Playbook ✓: BOJ normalization proceeding as telegraphed; yen weakness despite hikes is consistent with rate differentials.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The speed — from near-zero to 1% — is ahead of most institutional models. The yen's refusal to strengthen despite the fastest tightening in 30 years is the single biggest FX anomaly globally.
