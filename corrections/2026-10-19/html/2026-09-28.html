🌍 Geographic Macro Overview

🇺🇸 United States  Bearish  ↑ rates rising / hike risk

Macro Stance: The Fed is in a tightening or hawkish-hold posture, with officials openly discussing the prospect of another rate increase. The 10Y yield has surged to 5.25% (+53 bps vs 4W ago) while the 2Y sits at 4.81% (prior close, +61 bps), leaving a +36 bps curve that is positive but still flat — a bear flattening that screams policy repricing, not recession hedging. Fiscal dominance is the story — the deficit-funded AI buildout and energy capex are generating enough nominal growth to keep the economy running hot while bond vigilantes extract their pound of flesh. Oil at $92.99 (↑11.5%) is an additional inflation accelerant.

New / Most Important: Multiple Fed officials signaled they are not done hiking, with the NYT reporting the Fed "confronts the prospect of another rate increase ahead of midterms." Fitch warned that an AI spending collapse could tip the US into recession — a tail risk that highlights how dependent current GDP momentum is on a single capex vertical. The FT reported that soaring bond yields are "not even close" to cooling the economy, which is the most dangerous signal of all: fiscal impulse is overwhelming monetary restriction. Goldman leading a $37B bid for Palmer Square (CLO powerhouse) shows credit risk appetite remains ebullient despite the yield surge.

Playbook ✓: Mega-cap tech (Nasdaq 100 ↑2.9%) continues to outperform as a duration-insensitive cash-flow machine while small-caps (Russell 2000 ↓5.2%) get crushed by higher rates — the Russell/Nasdaq ratio collapsed 7.9%, textbook narrow-market behavior in a tightening cycle.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The structural break is that the US economy is now functionally ignoring rate levels that would have caused a recession in any prior cycle. This is the "fiscal dominance" regime that capital-flow frameworks have warned about: Treasury issuance is so large it creates its own demand (through deficit spending) even as it raises the cost of capital. The transmission mechanism is broken because the marginal borrower is the US government itself, which is price-insensitive, and because AI capex commitments are multi-year and largely equity-funded by mega-caps with fortress balance sheets. The danger is that this forces the Fed into an even more aggressive posture — potentially hiking into midterm elections — which would be politically explosive but monetarily necessary. Gold's 8.1% decline over 4 weeks is the tell: real rates are rising fast enough to dent even the structural debasement bid. The implication for global capital flows is severe — US yields at these levels are a vacuum for global savings, draining liquidity from every other market that can't match the risk-free return.

🇪🇺 European Union  Mixed  → rate plateau / uncertain

Macro Stance: The ECB is navigating a painful crosscurrent: an energy shock is keeping inflation elevated (Vujcic's explicit warning) while bank lending to eurozone businesses halved in August, signaling real credit contraction. Goldman sees rates unlikely to stay above 3%, suggesting the market expects eventual easing, but the path is blocked by supply-side inflation. Fiscal policy is constrained by the Stability Pact reinstatement.

New / Most Important: The halving of bank lending in August is a hard data point — this is a credit crunch in real time, even as "eurozone growth defies energy shock" per the headline flow. The ECB-BoC rate gap widened, strengthening the euro against the loonie, which signals diverging cycle positions across DM central banks. The ECB's Economic Bulletin and Vanguard outlook both point to a growth picture more resilient than expected, complicating the rate outlook.

Playbook ✓: Europe stuck in the familiar bind of too-hot inflation / too-weak credit, with energy dependence as the structural vulnerability.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The divergence is that growth is holding up despite the credit crunch — this suggests fiscal channels (defense spending, industrial policy) are substituting for private credit, a structural shift. If the ECB can't cut because of inflation but lending is already collapsing, the eurozone risks a "Japanese" outcome where policy rates stay elevated while the real economy bypasses the banking channel entirely. Watch European corporate bond spreads for the real stress signal.

🇨🇳 China  Mixed  → easing bias but constrained

Macro Stance: China remains in a structural deceleration overlaid with cyclical easing that runs through liquidity, not rates. The PBOC left the loan prime rates unchanged in September (www.tmgm.com, [Sep 21]), with the five-year rate at 3.5% (IDNFinancials, [Sep 21]), and with US 10Y yields at 5.25% the rate differential constrains how far it can cut. Instead it will offer banks up to 1 trillion yuan a day over the holidays (Bloomberg, [Sep 23]) and pledged to stay 'appropriately loose' (TradingView, [Sep 24]). The yuan is not the pressure point: it hit a fresh multi-year peak as the PBOC eased its curb (Reuters, [Sep 21]), the fix at 6.7468 against an estimate of 6.6971 (investingLive, [Sep 22]) marks the ceiling, and Societe Generale sees volatility rising as the truce runs to January (VT Markets, [Sep 26]). August CPI rose 0.8% and PPI 3.8% (BigGo Finance, [Sep 8]).

PMIs & Activity: The August official manufacturing PMI rebounded to 49.8 with new orders back in expansion (BigGo Finance, [Aug 30]), below 50 for a second month, while the private gauge rose to 51.5 (BigGo Finance, [Aug 31]). Reconciled: the private survey expands, the official one does not. The newest hard data confirm a narrow expansion: August industrial profits rose 4.2% (Briefs Finance, [Sep 27]), the weakest since the November slump (Bloomberg, [Sep 27]), leaving January-August up 15.7% with electronics contributing over 60% (BigGo Finance, [Sep 27]). Reuters says profit growth slowed further as economic imbalances deepen (Reuters, [Sep 27]). August output growth quickened to 5.2% (BigGo Finance, [Sep 14]), but retail sales rose only 0.4% (investingLive, [Sep 14]) and fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% in eight months (Xinhua, [Sep 15]); externally, a private survey showed a 'surprise' jump in US orders before the summit (CNBC, [Sep 25]).

Credit Impulse: August bank lending disappointed (Reuters, [Sep 14]) and credit expansion missed forecasts as loan demand sagged (Bloomberg, [Sep 14]), so loans and the aggregate do not conflict this month: both missed. The composition is the story: bond and equity financing exceeded loans in social financing for the first time (BigGo Finance, [Sep 14]), so issuance, not bank lending, carries the aggregate while households and firms decline to borrow. Regulators are narrowing consumer credit further, telling e-commerce apps to stop selling loans (Sixth Tone, [Sep 21]), and the one forced credit flow is defensive: banks were asked to keep Vanke loans off bad-debt books (Reuters, [Sep 22]).

Property: August home prices extended their decline across 70 cities, with tier-1 cities stabilising (BigGo Finance, [Sep 14]), and property investment fell 19.9% in eight months, the steepest decline on record (BigGo Finance, [Sep 14]). The marginal signs are better: first-tier new home sales jumped over 10% (BigGo Finance, [Sep 23]), Beijing now requires topped-out structures for presales (China Daily, [Sep 24]) and global investors see signs of recovery in China per JLL (South China Morning Post, [Sep 27]), while Hong Kong home prices were largely flat in August (Reuters, [Sep 27]). Property stocks surged on mortgage-subsidy rumours that analysts called unlikely to be implemented (BigGo Finance, [Sep 23]).

Fiscal, Policy & Trade: The summit's tangible outcome is a low-tariff regime on $60bn of goods (Financial Times, [Sep 27]), built from tariff cuts on $30 billion each and an AI dialogue (Reuters, [Sep 26]), with China to buy 10 million tons of US coal in 2027 and 2028 (Yahoo Finance, [Sep 26]). It sits on a truce extended by only two months (Reuters, [Sep 23]), Trump and Xi will meet twice more after the summit failed to resolve tensions (Financial Times, [Sep 26]), and China's continued control of rare earths loomed over the meeting (The New York Times, [Sep 22]). At home, policy is extracting rather than spending: the tax grab squeezes the rich elite harder than ever (Bloomberg, [Sep 27]) and October stimulus expectations are cooling (BigGo Finance, [Sep 25]) ahead of the Central Committee plenum set for late October (ThePrint, [Sep 21]).

Markets & Flows: Chinese stocks hit a one-year low as chip and optical firms slid (Yahoo Finance, [Sep 27]) and the Hang Seng slid 1% as tech lost steam (BusinessToday Malaysia, [Sep 27]); consumer stocks face a lost decade as AI steals the spotlight (Bloomberg, [Sep 26]). Reserves keep moving into gold, with a record of over 1,000 tonnes imported this year (Financial Times, [Sep 22]).

New / Most Important: August industrial profits slowed to 4.2%, the weakest since November, and electronics supplied over 60% of the year's gains. That is the growth pulse in one number: an AI and export engine on a sub-50 PMI, with consumer-facing industry left behind, and it arrived with Chinese stocks at a one-year low. The $60bn tariff regime helps the export engine at the margin; it does nothing for the domestic demand that profits, retail sales and credit all say is missing.

Playbook ✓: The 'managed deceleration' narrative holds: Beijing drip-feeds liquidity without delivering the bazooka, keeping growth above its floor while tolerating slower credit creation, and the US yield advantage keeps foreign money underweight. Key data on China is due this week per ING (ing think, [Sep 24]).  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The data now split along the AI line: electronics profits and US orders up, consumer stocks and retail sales down, property investment still falling 19.9%. That transmits abroad unevenly. Asian tech supply chains share the AI leg; the AUD and iron ore do not, because the demand they need sits in property and credit, both still contracting, as the RBA hikes. European autos face Chinese competition rather than Chinese demand, and rare-earth leverage over their supply chains stayed off the summit table. WTI's 11.5% four-week run is a supply shock that squeezes the consumer-facing margins China is already losing.

🇯🇵 Japan  Bearish (bonds) / Bullish (yen)  ↑ rates rising / hike imminent

Macro Stance: The BoJ is now in an active tightening cycle, having already hiked in September and with October back-to-back hike risk explicitly flagged by a former BoJ official. Japan's 2Y yield is nearing 2%, a level not seen in over a decade. The yen is strengthening on rate hike expectations and intervention warnings.

New / Most Important: July minutes revealed internal debate about the need for "faster rate hikes." An ex-BoJ official stated an October hike is a "real possibility." The JGB sell-off has been severe enough to stall the repatriation rush — Reuters reported Japan's bond "falling knife" is disrupting the flow mechanics. This is structurally important: if Japanese institutions can't repatriate because the JGB market is in freefall, the global liquidity drain that everyone feared from BoJ normalization gets delayed even as policy tightens.

Playbook ✓: Yen strengthening as rate differentials narrow — textbook.  |  ⚠️ Divergence: The "falling knife" dynamic in JGBs is the critical non-consensus observation. The market assumed BoJ tightening → repatriation → selling of USTs and European bonds → global yield contagion. Instead, the JGB sell-off itself is so violent that Japanese lifers and banks are paralyzed — they can't buy domestic bonds at rapidly moving prices and are stuck holding foreign assets longer than expected. This means the BoJ tightening cycle is paradoxically slowing the capital repatriation it was supposed to trigger, providing an accidental support to US Treasuries that the market hasn't priced. If the BoJ pauses to stabilize JGBs, the yen rally reverses; if it hikes again, the JGB "falling knife" deepens. It's a policy trap with no clean exit.

The housing channel is uniquely dangerous in Australia given household leverage — this could be the cycle where the "Australia has never had a recession" streak finally breaks.
